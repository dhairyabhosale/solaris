===============================================================================
1. WHAT PROBLEM DOES THIS SOLVE?
===============================================================================

Electricity cannot be stored cheaply at grid scale. Whatever India consumes at
2pm has to be generated at 2pm. So grid operators must decide TODAY how much
generation to line up for TOMORROW, hour by hour.

If they plan too little, there are blackouts. If they plan too much, expensive
plants run for nothing and money is wasted.

Demand is not constant. It rises when it is hot, because hundreds of millions
of fans and air conditioners switch on. It falls on holidays. It peaks in the
evening when people get home and, at the same moment, solar generation drops
to zero as the sun sets.

So the question this project asks is:

    "If we know what the weather forecast says for tomorrow, can we predict
     tomorrow's electricity demand more accurately than if we ignore it?"

And the follow-up:

    "Does the weather help MORE on hot days, which is exactly when the grid
     is under the most strain?"

The answer to both is yes, and this project measures by how much.


===============================================================================
2. WHERE DOES THE DATA COME FROM?
===============================================================================

TWO SOURCES. Both free, both open, no API keys needed.

(a) ELECTRICITY DATA - Grid-India

    Grid-India is the government body that runs India's power grid. Their
    North-Eastern Regional Load Despatch Centre published a dataset of actual
    grid measurements on Mendeley Data (a research data repository) under a
    CC BY 4.0 licence, which allows reuse with credit.

    It covers 1 September 2021 to 31 December 2023 and contains, for the
    whole of India: total electricity demand, and how much came from solar,
    wind, thermal (coal), hydro, gas and nuclear.

(b) WEATHER DATA - Open-Meteo

    Open-Meteo is a free weather API. Crucially, it keeps an archive of OLD
    FORECASTS - not just what the weather turned out to be, but what the
    forecast SAID it would be, on the day before.

    That distinction is the single most important idea in this project. More
    on it in section 5.

    I pulled weather for 18 major Indian cities spanning all five regional
    grids: Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Ahmedabad,
    Pune, Surat, Jaipur, Lucknow, Ludhiana, Nagpur, Indore, Coimbatore,
    Patna, Bhubaneswar and Guwahati.

WHAT ONE ROW OF MY FINAL DATASET MEANS

    One row = one hour, in Indian Standard Time.

    For example, the row for "2023-09-01 11:00 IST" says:
      - India used 239,406 megawatts of electricity in that hour
      - 41,476 MW of it came from solar, 2,571 MW from wind
      - the population-weighted temperature forecast for that hour was X °C
      - it was a Friday, not a holiday

    There are 20,448 such rows. That is 2 years and 4 months of hours, with
    no gaps at all.

SOMETHING I FOUND BY ACTUALLY OPENING THE FILES

    The dataset's title says the data is "at 1-hour interval". That is wrong.

    When I opened the files, each one had a hidden settings sheet recording
    the real sampling rate. The early files sample every 5 MINUTES. The files
    from October 2022 onwards sample every 10 SECONDS - which is why a single
    month's file is 48 MB with 267,840 rows.

    So I wrote code that reads that hidden setting, records it, and averages
    everything properly up to one hour. I also found three columns that are
    actually Excel FORMULAS, not numbers, so I recompute those myself from
    the raw measurements rather than trusting them.

    This matters if someone asks "did you just download a CSV?". No - I
    verified the data and found the published description was inaccurate.

3. WHAT DOES THE CLEANING AND FEATURE ENGINEERING DO?
===============================================================================

CLEANING - three real problems I had to fix:

  1. Negative solar at night. The solar sensor reads about -20 MW at
     midnight. The sun is not producing negative electricity; it is a sensor
     calibration offset. I clip it to zero, and keep the original column too
     so anyone can check what I did.

  2. Sensor blackouts. 17 hours out of 20,448 show impossible values - 12 of
     them read exactly 0 MW for the entire country, which obviously did not
     happen. These are SCADA dropouts. I flag them and exclude them from
     modelling, rather than quietly deleting them. That is 0.08% of the data.

  3. Timezone. The raw timestamps have no timezone attached. Grid-India runs
     on Indian Standard Time, and India has no daylight saving, so I attach
     IST directly. No ambiguity is possible.

FEATURE ENGINEERING - turning raw data into things a model can learn from

  "Features" are just the input columns I give the model. I build 37 of them
  in three groups.

  CALENDAR features - what day and hour is it?
     Hour of day, day of week, weekend or not, month, national holiday or
     not. I also encode hour as a sine and cosine pair, so the model knows
     that 11pm and midnight are next to each other rather than 23 units
     apart.

  HISTORY features - what has demand been doing recently?
     Demand at this same hour yesterday. And last week. The average for this
     hour over the past 7 days, and how much it has been bouncing around.
     Yesterday's daily average and daily peak.

  WEATHER features - what is the forecast saying?
     The forecast temperature, of course. But also:

       "Cooling degree hours": how far above 24 °C it is. Why? Because the
       relationship is not a straight line. Going from 10 °C to 15 °C changes
       electricity demand barely at all. Going from 35 °C to 40 °C changes it
       enormously, because that is when everyone turns the AC up. This
       feature lets the model capture that bend.

       "Three-day heat accumulation": how hot it has been over the last three
       days. Why? Because buildings store heat. The third hot day in a row
       uses more electricity than the first hot day, even at the same
       temperature, because the concrete never cooled down overnight.

       "Daily temperature range": a day that peaks at 40 °C but cools to 22 °C
       at night is easier on the grid than one that peaks at 40 °C and only
       falls to 32 °C.

       Per-region temperatures for all five regional grids, because a hot
       north with a mild south is a different situation from uniform warmth.

4. WHAT DOES THE MODEL PREDICT - AND WHAT DOES IT *NOT* MEAN?
===============================================================================

WHAT IT PREDICTS

  For each of the 24 hours of tomorrow, it predicts how many megawatts India
  will consume in that hour.

  It is a "day-ahead" forecast. The rule I set is: pretend it is 9am today,
  and predict every hour of tomorrow using only information that genuinely
  existed at 9am today.

WHAT IT DOES *NOT* MEAN

  - It does NOT mean temperature CAUSES a specific amount of demand. It
    means temperature forecasts carry useful INFORMATION about demand. Hot
    weather also coincides with summer, with different working patterns, with
    agricultural irrigation seasons. I have not separated those out, and I
    say so everywhere.

  - It is NOT an operational tool. I evaluated it on 2023 data. It has never
    been tested in real grid operations and nobody should despatch a power
    plant on it.

  - It does NOT predict 2024, 2025 or 2026. My data stops on 31 December
    2023. I make no claim about recent demand records.

  - The uncertainty band is NOT reliable. I'll come back to this honestly in
    section 7, because it is a real weakness and interviewers respect you
    knowing your own weaknesses.


===============================================================================
5. THE MOST IMPORTANT IDEA: NOT CHEATING (DATA LEAKAGE)
===============================================================================

If you remember one thing for your viva, remember this section.

"Data leakage" means accidentally giving your model information it could not
possibly have had at prediction time. It makes your results look brilliant
and makes them worthless.

THE TRAP MOST PEOPLE FALL INTO

  You want to predict tomorrow's demand using tomorrow's temperature. So you
  download historical weather and feed in the temperature that was actually
  recorded tomorrow.

  But a real forecaster does not know tomorrow's actual temperature. They
  only have tomorrow's FORECAST, which is wrong by a degree or two.

  If you train on actual temperature, your model looks great in testing and
  falls apart in reality, because in reality that column will never be
  available.

WHAT I DID INSTEAD

  Open-Meteo has a "Previous Runs" API that stores old forecasts. I can ask
  it: "On 14 May 2022, what did you predict for 3pm on 15 May 2022?"

  That is the number a real forecaster would have had. That is what I use.

  I checked that they genuinely differ. For Delhi on 15 May 2022, the
  day-ahead forecast for the afternoon said 45.4, 46.3, 46.7, 46.9 °C. The
  actual recorded values were 44.6, 44.9, 45.2, 45.4 °C. So the forecast ran
  about 1.5 °C too warm - a real forecast with real error, not a relabelled
  observation.

THREE MORE LEAKAGE RULES I ENFORCE IN CODE

  1. No demand lag shorter than 24 hours. If it is 9am today and I am
     predicting 11pm tomorrow, I do not know what demand was at 10pm
     tomorrow. So the shortest history I allow is "same hour yesterday".

  2. No same-hour grid measurements. I know how much solar India generated at
     2pm yesterday, but I do not know how much it will generate at 2pm
     tomorrow. So solar, wind and generation at the target hour are banned as
     inputs. I only use them to DESCRIBE what happened, never to predict.

  3. No random train/test splitting. With time series you must split by time.
     If you shuffle randomly, you train on December and test on November -
     your model has literally seen the future.

  I wrote a function called assert_no_leakage() that enforces all of this and
  throws an error if any banned column sneaks in. There are tests for it.

  There is also a clever test I am proud of: it recomputes every history
  feature on a truncated copy of the data, and checks the values are
  identical. If any feature were secretly peeking at the future, cutting the
  future off would change it. It does not.


===============================================================================
6. WHAT IS A BASELINE, AND WHY DOES COMPARING MATTER?
===============================================================================

A baseline is the dumbest sensible method. If your fancy model cannot beat
it, your fancy model is pointless.

MY MAIN BASELINE: "same hour yesterday"

  To predict 3pm tomorrow, just say "whatever it was at 3pm today."

  That sounds stupid. It is not. Electricity demand is very smooth and very
  habitual. This baseline gets a mean error of 4,938 MW - about 2.7% off.
  Lots of published forecasting work fails to beat this.

MY OTHER BASELINES

  - Same hour LAST WEEK: much worse (9,807 MW). Weather changes in a week.
  - Hour-and-weekday average: terrible (22,386 MW). It ignores the fact that
    India's demand grew substantially over the period.
  - Ridge regression on calendar and history: 4,662 MW. A respectable linear
    model.

WHY I HAVE SO MANY

  Because the honest question is not "is my model good?" but "is my model
  better than the obvious alternatives, and by how much?"

  My best model gets 3,398 MW - 31.2% better than the naive baseline.


===============================================================================
7. WHAT DO THE EVALUATION METRICS MEAN IN PLAIN ENGLISH?
===============================================================================

MAE (Mean Absolute Error) = 3,398 MW
    On average, my forecast is wrong by about 3,400 megawatts in either
    direction. India's demand averages around 185,000 MW, so that is small.
    "Absolute" means I ignore the direction of the error, so over- and
    under-predictions do not cancel out.

MAPE (Mean Absolute Percentage Error) = 1.87%
    The same thing as a percentage. My forecast is typically within about
    1.9% of the true value. This is the number to quote if someone wants one
    figure, because it does not need context about India's grid size.

RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error) = 4,565 MW
    Like MAE, but it squares the errors first, which punishes big mistakes
    much more harshly. RMSE is always bigger than MAE. If RMSE is MUCH bigger
    than MAE, you have some very large occasional errors. Mine is 4,565 vs
    3,398 - a normal ratio, so no catastrophic outliers.

BIAS = -615 MW
    The average error WITH direction. Negative means I systematically
    under-predict slightly. Worth knowing: a grid operator would rather you
    erred high.

R-squared = 0.945
    The fraction of the variation in demand my model explains. 1.0 would be
    perfect, 0 would be no better than always guessing the average. 0.945 is
    strong.

PEAK MAE = 3,281 MW, PEAK HOUR MAE = 1.63 hours
    How wrong I am about the highest demand hour of each day, and about WHEN
    it happens. I get the peak hour exactly right on 50.7% of days and within
    one hour on 77.0%.

p-VALUE = 0.00000000032
    From a paired statistical test. It answers: "could this improvement be
    luck?" A p-value this tiny means essentially no - the chance of seeing an
    improvement this consistent by luck is about 3 in 10 billion. I ran the
    test on DAILY errors rather than hourly, because hourly errors are
    correlated with each other and would overstate my confidence.

8. WHAT ARE THE ACTUAL RESULTS?
===============================================================================

TEST PERIOD: 1 March 2023 to 31 December 2023 (7,344 hours).
These hours were never used for training, tuning, or anything else.

MAIN RESULT

    Naive baseline (same hour yesterday)    4,938 MW
    My model WITHOUT weather                3,822 MW
    My model WITH weather                   3,398 MW   <-- best

    Weather improves the forecast by 11.07%.
    The best model is 31.2% better than the naive baseline.

THE HEADLINE FINDING - WEATHER HELPS MOST WHEN IT MATTERS MOST

    On research-defined hot hours:   21.45% improvement from weather
    On all other hours:               9.32% improvement from weather

    That is the finding worth leading with. Weather information is more than
    twice as valuable during hot periods - exactly when the grid is most
    stressed and a forecast error is most expensive.

    And it is not an artefact of where I drew the "hot" line. I tested four
    thresholds: the improvement was 15.7%, 18.8%, 21.5% and 22.6%.

IS IT JUST LUCK? NO

    p = 0.00000000032 on a paired test over 305 days.
    65.6% of individual days improved.
    I also refitted the model fresh every month for 12 months (a "rolling
    backtest") and weather helped in 9 of those 12 months.

EVENING GRID STRESS

    Looking at "residual load" - demand minus solar and wind, which is what
    conventional plants must cover:

      - The steepest hour-to-hour climb averages 12.4 GW per hour
      - One day in twenty it exceeds 17.8 GW per hour
      - Over three hours it averages 26.2 GW
      - It most often peaks at 19:00 IST

    To picture 12.4 GW/h: that is like switching on roughly a dozen large
    power stations within a single hour, every single evening, because the
    sun went down while everyone got home.

THE NEGATIVE RESULT I KEPT

    Two of my models are WORSE than the naive baseline, and I report them
    anyway because the reason is genuinely interesting.

    A decision-tree model can only ever output an average of values it saw
    during training. It literally cannot predict a number higher than its
    training maximum.

    India's demand grew a lot. My training data maxes out at 209.8 GW. But
    the test period reaches 239.4 GW, and 526 test hours are above anything
    the model ever saw. So that model hits a ceiling at 204.7 GW and just
    stops.

    THE FIX: instead of predicting "tomorrow's demand will be 220 GW", I
    predict "tomorrow will be 3 GW higher than today was", then add today's
    actual value. That number - the CHANGE - is stable over time even though
    the LEVEL keeps growing. Now the ceiling is gone: the fixed model reaches
    237.2 GW.

    This is a great thing to be asked about. It shows you understand what
    your algorithm actually does rather than treating it as a black box.

9. WHAT IS FEATURE IMPORTANCE / SHAP, AND WHAT DOES IT NOT PROVE?
===============================================================================

WHAT IT IS

  The model makes thousands of yes/no splits ("is the temperature above
  31 °C?"). Feature importance adds up how much each input improved the
  model's accuracy across all those splits. It tells you what the model
  leans on.

MY TOP FEATURES

    demand_lag_24h               36%   (demand at this hour yesterday)
    dayofweek                    12%
    demand_same_hour_std_7d       8%   (how volatile this hour has been)
    hour                          8%
    demand_trend_1d_7d            7%   (yesterday vs last week)
    temp_fc_anomaly_vs_prev_day   4%   (how much warmer than yesterday)

  Yesterday's demand dominates, which makes complete sense for a 24-hour
  forecast. The most useful WEATHER feature is not raw temperature but the
  CHANGE in temperature from yesterday - which fits perfectly, because my
  model is predicting the change in demand from yesterday.

WHAT IT DOES *NOT* PROVE

  - It does not prove causation. It shows statistical usefulness to this
    particular model, nothing more.
  - When two inputs are correlated (temperature and cooling degree hours very
    much are), they split the credit arbitrarily. A low score can mean "not
    useful" OR "useful but another column already covers it".
  - A different model trained on the same data could rank them differently.

  If asked "so temperature causes 4% of demand?" - no. Say: "It means the
  temperature-change feature contributed about 4% of the model's total
  predictive gain. Establishing causation would need a completely different
  study design."

11. HOW DO I RUN AND REPRODUCE EVERYTHING?
===============================================================================

    pip install -r requirements-research.txt    # full pipeline
    # (requirements.txt is the small web-app-only set, see note below)

    python -m solaris.cli fetch-grid       # downloads 713 MB, slow
    python -m solaris.cli fetch-weather    # weather for 18 cities
    python -m solaris.cli prepare          # clean and join, ~8 minutes
    python -m solaris.cli train --rolling  # fit and score everything
    python -m solaris.cli report           # rebuild charts and tables

    pytest                                 # 108 tests, all offline
    uvicorn app.main:app --reload          # launch dashboard

A NOTE ON THE TWO REQUIREMENTS FILES

  requirements-research.txt  - everything: pandas, LightGBM, SHAP, SciPy...
  requirements.txt           - just four packages, for the dashboard only

  Why split them? The dashboard doesn't do any modelling. It reads a small
  JSON file that the pipeline already wrote, using Python's built-in library.
  So the deployed website doesn't need LightGBM or pandas at all - which takes
  the deployment from about 250 MB down to 35 MB and makes it start instantly.

  If an interviewer asks about deployment, that's a good detail to mention:
  separating the research stack from the serving stack is normal practice in
  production ML, and here it is enforced by a test that fails if the web code
  ever imports pandas.

Everything the project claims lives in reports/evaluation.json, written by
the training command. The README, the docs, the charts and the dashboard all
read from it. Nothing is typed in by hand. If you re-ran the pipeline and the
numbers changed, every one of those would change with it.

12. THE 30-SECOND EXPLANATION
===============================================================================

"India's grid operators have to decide today how much electricity to generate
tomorrow, hour by hour. I built a machine learning system that forecasts
tomorrow's national demand using the weather forecast - and critically, using
the forecast as it existed yesterday, not the weather that actually happened,
because that is all a real forecaster would have.

On ten months of held-out data it averages 1.87% error, which is 31% better
than the standard industry baseline. The interesting finding is that weather
information helps more than twice as much during hot periods - 21% versus 9% -
which is exactly when the grid is under most strain."


===============================================================================
13. THE 2-MINUTE EXPLANATION
===============================================================================

"Electricity can't be stored at grid scale, so supply has to match demand
every single hour. India's grid operators commit generation a day in advance,
and demand swings hugely with temperature because of air conditioning load.

So I asked: does knowing tomorrow's weather forecast actually make day-ahead
demand forecasts better, and does it help more when it's hot?

For data I used Grid-India's SCADA measurements - actual national demand and
generation - published through Mendeley under CC BY. Two and a bit years,
20,448 hours. Worth mentioning: the dataset is titled 'hourly' but when I
opened the files it's actually 5-minute and 10-second SCADA exports, so I
wrote the aggregation myself and documented the discrepancy.

For weather, the key methodological point. Most projects would use the
temperature that was actually recorded. That's data leakage - a real
forecaster doesn't know tomorrow's temperature, only tomorrow's forecast. So I
used Open-Meteo's archive of old forecast runs, which gives me exactly what a
forecaster would have had the previous day. I also enforce it in code: there's
a leakage-guard function that rejects observed weather, same-hour grid
measurements, and any demand lag under 24 hours.

I split chronologically - train on 2021-22, validate on early 2023, test on
March to December 2023. I deliberately placed the test block to include a hot
season, because an earlier split I tried had zero hot hours in the test
period, which would have made the central question unanswerable.

Then I compared everything against baselines. The naive 'same hour yesterday'
baseline gets 4,938 MW error. My model without weather gets 3,822. With
weather, 3,398 - an 11% improvement, p-value around 3 times 10 to the minus
10, confirmed across a 12-fold rolling backtest.

The headline finding is the hot-period split: 21.5% improvement on hot hours
versus 9.3% otherwise, robust across four different thresholds.

I also kept a negative result. My first tree models lost to the baseline,
because a gradient-boosted tree can't predict above its training maximum and
Indian demand grew past it - 526 test hours were above anything it had seen.
I fixed it by predicting the day-on-day change rather than the level, but I
report both, because the failure explains why the fix was necessary."

14. LIKELY INTERVIEW QUESTIONS, WITH HONEST ANSWERS
===============================================================================

Q: Why LightGBM and not deep learning?
A: Twenty thousand rows is small for a neural network - it would overfit the
   calendar. Gradient boosting is the standard strong choice for tabular data
   this size and it trains in seconds, so I could run proper sensitivity
   analyses and a 12-fold backtest. I'd consider an LSTM with several more
   years of data. Adding deep learning just to list it would have been worse
   engineering, not better.

Q: Only 11% improvement from weather - isn't that small?
A: Two things. First, it's measured against a strong baseline that already
   has demand history, which carries a lot of weather signal implicitly -
   yesterday's demand already encodes yesterday's weather. Second, 11% is the
   average; on hot hours it's 21.5%, and that's where it matters. It's also
   almost certainly a lower bound, because only temperature was available at
   a true day-ahead lead - no humidity, which matters a lot for Indian
   cooling load.

Q: Why is your test period longer than your training period?
A: A deliberate trade-off. India's hot season is March to June. My first split
   trained through April 2023 and tested July to December, and the test block
   had literally zero hot hours - which made the main research question
   unanswerable. So I moved the boundary. Sixteen months of training still
   covers a complete annual cycle, and the rolling backtest is the check that
   the single split isn't a fluke.

Q: How do you know you don't have data leakage?
A: Three ways. There's a guard function that rejects observed weather,
   same-hour grid measurements and demand lags under 24 hours, and it's
   tested. There's a test that recomputes every history feature on truncated
   data and asserts nothing changes - if anything peeked forward, that test
   would fail. And the weather inputs come from an archived-forecast API, not
   a historical-weather API, which I verified by checking that the forecast
   and the observation genuinely differ.

Q: Your uncertainty band doesn't work. Why ship it?
A: Because I'd rather report a measured failure than hide it. The P10-P90
   band covers 57.5% of actuals against a nominal 80%. I diagnosed why: on
   the validation block, where it was tuned, it covers 72%, so it's roughly
   right there and too narrow out of sample. The validation block is January
   and February, with a mean daily demand range of 58.9 GW versus 34.7 GW on
   test. That's distribution shift. The fix would be recalibrating on a window
   that spans a hot season. I labelled it as not fit for use as a risk band.

Q: Is this good enough for a real grid operator?
A: No, and I say so in the model card. It's evaluated on historical data,
   never tested in live operations, and it slightly under-predicts peaks,
   which is the wrong direction for an operator. It's a research artefact
   demonstrating the method, not a product.

Q: Did temperature cause the demand increase?
A: I can't say that and I don't. I've shown predictive association:
   temperature forecasts carry information that improves demand prediction.
   Temperature is confounded with season, working patterns, agricultural
   irrigation and economic activity, and I have no identification strategy to
   separate those. Causal estimation would be a different study.

Q: Why population weights for the weather index?
A: Because I couldn't obtain actual regional demand weights in a reproducible
   machine-readable form from Grid-India or CEA, and I wasn't willing to make
   numbers up. Population is a transparent, citable proxy - Census 2011 urban
   agglomerations. It understates industrial and agricultural load, which is a
   real weakness. I mitigated it by re-running everything under three
   weighting schemes; weather wins under all three, at 11.1%, 10.5% and 7.3%.
   So the conclusion is robust but the magnitude does depend on the choice.

Q: What would you do next?
A: Three things, in order. Find a humidity forecast at a genuine day-ahead
   lead, because wet-bulb temperature likely matters more than dry-bulb for
   Indian cooling load. Recalibrate the quantiles properly, probably with
   conformal prediction on a rolling window. And extend past 2023 to test
   whether the relationship is stable as rooftop solar grows, since
   behind-the-meter solar is invisible in this data and is changing the shape
   of the evening ramp.

Q: What was the hardest part?
A: Realising my first results were wrong for a structural reason. The tree
   models were losing to a naive baseline and my instinct was to tune
   hyperparameters. The actual cause was that gradient-boosted trees can't
   extrapolate, and Indian demand had grown past the training range. No
   amount of tuning fixes that - it needed a change in what the model
   predicts. That taught me to diagnose before I tune.
