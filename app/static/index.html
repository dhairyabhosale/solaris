Solaris Grid Intelligence

Day-ahead demand forecasting for India

Predicting tomorrow's electricity demand from today's weather forecast.

India's grid operators must decide today how much power to generate for every hour of tomorrow. Get it wrong and you either waste money or risk blackouts. Solaris tests whether the weather forecast makes that decision measurably better.

Start here What this is, in plain English

What it is

A machine learning system that predicts how much electricity the whole of India will use, hour by hour, one day in advance. It was built and tested on two and a half years of real grid measurements from Grid-India.

What it does

It takes the weather forecast for tomorrow, plus how much power the country used recently and what day of the week it is, and produces a demand figure for each of the next 24 hours. It also shows when the evening crunch will hit.

Why it matters

Electricity cannot be stored cheaply at national scale, so supply has to match demand every hour. A better forecast means fewer expensive standby plants burning fuel for nothing, and less risk of falling short on a hot evening.

How something like this gets used in real life

  • Grid operators Decide which power stations to switch on for tomorrow. Being 400 MW closer to the truth across a day removes roughly one mid-sized plant's worth of guesswork.
  • Power traders Day-ahead electricity markets price every hour separately. A sharper demand view informs what to buy or sell, and at what price.
  • Renewable planners Solar output collapses at sunset while demand climbs. Knowing how steep that evening ramp will be shows how much battery or flexible generation is needed.
  • Heat preparedness Hot spells drive air-conditioning load. Spotting the hardest days a day ahead gives time to prepare instead of react.

To be clear about what this is: a research project evaluated on historical data, not a live operational tool. The numbers on this site are real and measured, but nobody should despatch a power station on them.

How national demand moves with temperature, and whether weather information helps the forecast more when it is hot.

Association, not causation. These charts show that hot days and high demand happen together, and that temperature forecasts improve the model. They do not prove that temperature causes a given amount of demand. Temperature also tracks the season, working patterns and economic activity, and nothing here separates those out.

    Reproduce it yourself

    git clone https://github.com/dhairyabhosale/solaris
cd solaris
pip install -r requirements-research.txt

python -m solaris.cli fetch-grid      # about 713 MB from Mendeley
python -m solaris.cli fetch-weather   # archived forecasts, Open-Meteo
python -m solaris.cli prepare         # parse, join, quality-check
python -m solaris.cli train --rolling # fit and evaluate every model
python -m solaris.cli report          # rebuild figures and tables

uvicorn app.main:app --reload

    Every figure and table on this site is read from the files those commands write. Nothing here is typed in by hand.
